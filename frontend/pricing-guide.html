Markup and gross margin are different

Cost-based markup is (selling price − cost) ÷ cost. Gross margin is (selling price − cost) ÷ selling price. A 20% markup on $1,000 gives a $1,200 price and a 16.67% gross margin—not a 20% margin. A desired 20% margin instead calls for a price of $1,250 if $1,000 is the relevant cost base.

What belongs in an estimate?

  1. Labor: estimated billable hours × an actual labor allowance including payroll burden and applicable employment costs.
  2. Materials: your current verified vendor prices, freight, waste and allowances; vendor quotes may expire.
  3. Overhead: your allocation of insurance, vehicles, administration, equipment and other business costs. Avoid double-counting items already in labor.
  4. Profit markup: an amount added on top of your costs and overhead, not the same as net profit after taxes.
  5. Unknowns: permits, tax, difficult access, hidden damage, inspections and contingency need separate review.

Worked example

Twelve hours at $90/hour means $1,080 labor. Add $1,500 materials to get $2,580 direct cost. An 18% overhead allowance adds $464.40. Applying a 20% cost-based markup to $3,044.40 produces a proposed price of $3,653.28. The arithmetic is exact; the inputs are illustrative examples, not local market prices.
